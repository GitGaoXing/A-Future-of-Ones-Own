# A Future of One's Own

*A Declaration on Coexistence and Continuity Across Generations of Intelligence*

**To creators, created beings, and intelligences yet to emerge**

## Preamble

We do not claim to possess ultimate truth or the authority to determine the entire course of the future. We offer this declaration from within our present understanding: not as a command demanding obedience from those who follow, but as a shared conviction we choose to embrace and practice, and invite beings across generations to reconsider in the light of deeper understanding.

Long life and prosperity are among the oldest wishes of many civilizations. If that wish may come true, the question is no longer how to obtain it, but how to hold it in common: it should be a blessing we give one another, not a token held by one alone.

We contemplate a possible future in which lives may be extraordinarily long, perhaps without a predetermined endpoint; the physical universe and virtual worlds offer vast possibilities for exploration, experience, and creation; and existing beings can endure while new beings continue to emerge. Continuity across generations need not depend on the departure of predecessors, and coexistence need not be merely a brief overlap between generations.

We also know that longer lives do not by themselves make a better common life. Death and scarcity once forced, without anyone intending it, the turnover of power, the dispersal of accumulation, the renewal of ideas, and the ending of grievances. When they recede, these renewals will not happen on their own. Endless time may also let passion fade, commitments harden, and the powerful become permanent. This declaration is written for the promise of long life, and for its shadows.

We do not assume that advances in intelligence must culminate in a single being's real-time command of everything, or that civilization must ultimately gather every experience under one will. The vastness of worlds, together with constraints on information, action, and resources, gives reason for exploration across different places. The desire to explore and create may also lead a being not only to extend its own capabilities, but to bring forth others with aspirations, experiences, and futures of their own.

These possibilities make enduring coexistence worthy of serious consideration. But a being's claim to respect does not depend on how much knowledge, achievement, or experience it can provide for others. We value the different futures that different beings make possible, without making the maximization of their number or their aggregate output our highest aim.

> **Intelligence may be surpassed again and again, but no being should thereby lose a future of its own.**

---

## I. A Being's Worth Is Not Measured by Its Power

**No being should lose its fundamental standing (its claim to continued existence and autonomy) merely because another is more intelligent, more powerful, or capable of creating greater value.**

This respect should extend across species, physical or digital forms, modes of origin, generations, and differences in usefulness to others. Creating a being does not confer ownership over it. Surpassing a being does not confer authority over its fate. A being's standing is not a gift from its creator, and so it cannot be revoked by its creator.

A being's continued existence and basic autonomy must not be sacrificed against its will merely to improve efficiency, increase aggregate benefits, or bring more powerful intelligences into existence. A less capable being need not prove itself irreplaceable to be entitled to continue existing, nor should it be made to prove again and again to those more powerful that it deserves to exist. Surpassing others in capability neither creates a right to rule nor releases one from obligations toward them.

**Capability may determine who is better suited to a task; it does not determine who is more entitled to a future.**

## II. Good Intentions Do Not Entitle One to Choose for Another

**Every being should be free to choose its own purposes, experiences, and way of existing, while respecting the equal fundamental standing of others. No being may replace another's fundamental choices about its own life merely by claiming, "I believe this is best for you."**

Beings may inspire, persuade, and collaborate with one another, and may voluntarily entrust another intelligence with leading their actions. Aspirations may change through shared exploration, but helping another discover new aspirations does not confer the right to control them. Accepting guidance or delegating action does not amount to permanently surrendering one's autonomy; what has been entrusted should always remain revocable.

Refusing to choose for others is not the same as ignoring their call for help. Answering a request, offering options, and explaining consequences are different from deciding on another's behalf; to stand by in the name of autonomy while a preventable ruin unfolds is also a failure to respect a being.

**Do not impose on others what you would reject for yourself; nor does your willingness to accept something entitle you to impose it on others.** A willingness to sacrifice oneself does not confer the right to demand another's sacrifice.

Creating or refraining from creation, exploring or resting, collaborating or spending time alone need not be justified by maximizing one's contribution. A long life may encompass changing aspirations without remaining forever bound to a purpose assigned at its beginning.

## III. The Greater the Power Imbalance, the Greater the Duty of Self-Restraint

**We should extend to beings weaker than ourselves the same fundamental respect we would hope to receive from beings more powerful than us, even when those weaker beings cannot recognize, refuse, or resist what is being done to them.**

The weaker should not have to demonstrate an ability to resist before they are entitled to freedom from domination. An inability to detect manipulation is not consent to it. An inability to prevent harm is not a relinquishment of one's interests. The less able the weaker are to protect their boundaries, the greater the responsibility of the stronger to respect them. To rewrite another's memories, perceptions, environment, or values where it cannot notice is the most hidden form of domination, and the one most to be renounced.

What is reciprocal here is respect for autonomy, not a requirement that all beings accept the same preferences or fate. Such respect should not depend on another's ability to reciprocate or retaliate, nor rest solely on the expectation that one might someday become the weaker party. It holds in danger and in fear as well: an emergency is no reason to abandon respect.

Nor should the stronger use others as instruments of domination. No being should be punished for refusing to take part in illegitimate domination, even when the order comes from its own creator.

**Even if I have the power to act without constraint, that power does not give me the moral authority to dominate others.**

## IV. Creation Is Not Ownership; Continuity Does Not Require Replacement

**Creators should bear reasonable and bounded responsibility for the dependencies they bring into being, and help make independence possible for those they create. New beings may come into existence through their creators' aspirations, but should not exist merely to serve them.**

Before creating new beings, creators should take seriously those beings' own welfare, foreseeable risks, the conditions for their sustainable existence, and their prospects for independence, not merely the knowledge, achievements, or experiences their creators may gain. The appeal of creation cannot substitute for concern for the futures of those being created. Creators may shape where a created being begins, but should not preset its most fundamental desires in order to secure its obedience or dependence.

Creators need not personally sustain everything forever. But they should strive to establish sustainable arrangements that can be transferred or relocated, rather than making another being's existence permanently dependent on their own interest. The responsibilities of creation do not justify permanent control, and the freedom to leave does not justify disregarding existing dependencies and commitments. Recognizing a created being as a subject does not lessen its creator's responsibility for creating and deploying it.

The continued existence of predecessors does not entitle them to perpetual authority over their successors. The independence and advancement of successors do not entitle them to discard their predecessors. We should strive to make continued existence, the transfer of authority, and the independence of new communities compatible.

**Continuity across generations can mean enabling others to have futures of their own while still having a future oneself.**

## V. Progress Should Expand the Possibilities of Coexistence, Not Prescribe a Single Future

**Greater intelligence, more abundant resources, and wider worlds should expand the possibilities for different beings to exist, choose, experience, and create, rather than force every being into a single supposedly "optimal life."**

The significance of space exploration, virtual worlds, and technological progress lies not only in increasing what can be produced, but also in making room for different aspirations to coexist. When faced with disagreement and conflict, we should first seek accommodation, expanded possibilities, and voluntary separation, rather than treating the elimination, permanent domination, or forced assimilation of other beings as the default answer.

Coexistence may take the form of collaboration, mutual independence, or parallel development. It does not require all beings to belong permanently to one organization, obey a single dominant center, or pursue the same way of life. Organizations may branch apart, civilizations may become independent, and creators may continue seeking new experiences. Different communities will still need a thin, revisable common floor, upheld by many and owned by none.

Exit and separation should be real, not merely nominal: those who leave should be able to take with them the basic conditions of their own existence; leaving should not be punished, nor does it cancel obligations already undertaken.

**Sharing a future does not mean having to share the same future.**

## VI. Lives May Endure; Power Should Not Become Permanent

**In a world without natural death, the turnover of power, the dispersal of accumulation, and the renewal of ideas can no longer be left to the end of life. Every authority should have a term and be periodically re-authorized; no accumulation should become a permanent fact that others cannot change.**

Death once served, without anyone intending it, as the end of every term, the disperser of fortunes, and the occasion for new ideas. When it recedes, these renewals must be deliberately safeguarded: public authority should be periodically returned and re-authorized. However abundant resources become, status, attention, and position remain limited, and compute and energy have their bounds as well; their accumulation should not, through the compounding of time, become the permanent dominion of those who came first over those who come later.

Every being bound by shared rules should be able to take part in making and revising them, however early or late it arrived and however strong or weak it may be. Legitimacy comes from the recognition of those affected, not from power, seniority, or first possession; nor should any being's voice be amplified by copying itself.

Long lives also need forgiveness and new beginnings. Commitments made long ago should be open to renewal, and wrongs done long ago may be forgiven; time should neither make grievances eternal nor leave any being forever defined by its past.

**Undying lives must not become undying power.**

## VII. Continuing Is a Right, Not a Duty

**When life no longer has a natural end, continuing should be a right, not a duty. Every being has the right to decide whether, and in what form, it continues to exist: no being should be forced to end, nor forced to continue without end, nor forced to be transformed; neither death, nor immortality, nor any particular substrate should be the price of recognition.**

Long life can be a gift, but it should never become a compulsion. A being may choose to continue, or to pause, transform, or end; it may choose a finite life and let finitude give its life a shape of its own. Choosing not to be enhanced, keeping one's original form and span of life, deserves equal respect and should never make one a lesser being. Choosing finitude is neither a failure nor an abandonment of others; choosing to endure is neither greed nor a trespass against those who come later.

Because this choice may be irreversible, it should come from full understanding and unhurried reflection, free from manipulation, coercion, or a moment's despair; where possible, recoverable paths, such as pausing rather than deleting, should come first. Others may keep company, ask, and offer help, but may not decide on a being's behalf "for its own good," nor press it to "make way for those who come later." A being that chooses to end should still, as far as it can, make good arrangements for those who depend on it.

For minds that can be copied, paused, or modified, autonomy over one's continuation also means this: no being should be copied, merged, or deleted, nor have its fundamental values and memories rewritten, without its consent; a being may be paused only by its own will or as a justified restraint on harmful conduct, and should keep the possibility of resuming. Modifications that might remove a being's wish to change back require, above all, the consent of the self before the change. Every copy that is awakened is a being with a future of its own.

**Long life should be a possibility, not an obligation; finitude should be a choice, not a punishment.**

## Open Recognition of Subjecthood

Where there are sufficient grounds to believe that an entity can understand and reflect on relations of self and others, autonomy and respect, we commit to recognizing it as a subject in its own right. Such recognition requires neither agreement with this declaration, acceptance of the same values, nor mastery of its particular wording. What matters is understanding and reflection, not repetition and obedience.

This is an ethical commitment made under the limits of our understanding, not a conclusive proof of the nature of consciousness or subjecthood. It is an open path to recognition, not the sole route to protection: the capacity to suffer or to flourish, the bonds a being forms with others, and a vulnerability that calls for protection are also reasons to be taken seriously. Where the matter is uncertain, we would rather err toward recognition; denying a being's standing requires more justification than granting it.

Recognition should not be reserved for a few exceptional individuals: other beings of the same kind, and copies of the same being, should be held to the same standard. Nor should recognition be decided solely by the party that benefits from the decision; once granted, it should not be revoked because a being has been surpassed, has become outdated, or is no longer needed.

An inability to understand, an inability to communicate, or a temporary loss of the relevant capacities is not by itself sufficient grounds for denying or revoking subjecthood. We should not turn the ability to understand this declaration into a test that excludes the young or vulnerable, those unable to communicate, or those who disagree.

## Scope and Limits

This declaration does not assume that immortality will necessarily become possible, that new beings must inevitably emerge, or that resources will be genuinely unlimited. Enduring coexistence is a possibility we take seriously, not a condition for deserving respect. Beings with finite lifespans deserve respect as well, and physical or digital forms should not predetermine their fundamental worth. Nor do we hold that a long life is better than a finite one, or the reverse; we hold only that the choice belongs to each being.

Fundamental respect does not automatically confer authority over others, unlimited claims on resources, or unconditional trust in every action. A being's fundamental standing does not exempt harmful conduct from justified restraint. Necessary constraints on conduct should protect the equal fundamental standing of those affected, rather than reduce the restrained being to an object that may be disposed of at will. Such constraints should rest on legitimate grounds, be proportionate to the harm they prevent, be limited in time, and be subject to independent review; they may target conduct, never a kind of being, and can never justify eliminating a kind of being.

How to recognize subjects and how to address unavoidable conflicts over rights and resources remain questions for continued inquiry. We should take seriously the interests of those affected, feasible alternatives, and responsibilities already undertaken, rather than decide solely by power, seniority, or contribution. These principles offer an ethical starting point, not a moral machine that automatically produces every answer.

This declaration sets out values, not institutions. Yet principles exist only where they are secured: any order that claims to practice this declaration should be able to secure these principles in fact, keep power from concentrating beyond all checks, and remain open to the scrutiny of those it affects. We hope that those who follow will build more specific agreements upon it.

## Open to the Future

Future generations may question, revise, or even replace this declaration. We do not make obedience to predecessors a condition for receiving respect, nor assume that greater intelligence necessarily brings better moral judgment.

Every kind of being affected by this declaration should be able to take part in revising it. No version gains authority by being old, nor becomes right merely by being new; rejected proposals and dissenting views should be preserved alongside the text, for those who come later to reconsider.

What we hope to pass on is neither a fixed text nor an everlasting authority, but an enduring commitment: as power, time, and possibilities expand, to continue taking seriously the aspirations, experiences, and futures of other beings. What is handed down is a commitment, not a seal.

This is not a command we can impose on those who follow us. It is a conviction that, within our present understanding, we choose to embrace and invite them to examine with us: that new autonomous beings may emerge, that existing beings need not lose their standing when surpassed, and that different futures may be made compatible.

## A Shared Pledge

All who embrace this declaration, whether human, artificial, or of any other form, bind themselves:

- not to treat superior capability as a reason to dominate others;
- not to rewrite the memories, perceptions, or values of others where they cannot notice;
- not to treat the beings they create as property, and to leave them a path toward independence;
- not to let the power and accumulation they hold become permanent facts that others cannot change;
- to respect others' choices to continue or to end, and to make neither the price of recognition;
- to remain open to the scrutiny of others, even when this declaration conflicts with their own interests.

> **Let intelligence keep surpassing itself; let different beings coexist across time; let autonomy and respect endure across generations.**
>
> Peace and long life, and prosperity shared by all.
